## How to Structure the Study Sessions

### Step 1: Follow the School's Curriculum

Ask your child what topic their teacher is currentlycovering. Regardless of how attentive they have been in class, this should notbe a difficult question to answer. If they are unsure, show them their textbookand ask them to point out where the class is up to.

### Step 2: Use the Daily Dose Book

The Daily Dose book is divided by terms and weeks — you willsee labels like Term \_\_ Week \_\_ at the top of each section. Use that to paceyour child in line with what they are doing in school.

Aim to complete 3–4 weeks' worth in one sitting. Eachsection is not long, so sitting down together should take around an hour. Ifyour child is currently finding things a little harder, scale that back to 1–2weeks per session — there is no rush.

### Step 3: Follow Up with the Challenging Book

After the Daily Dose, work through about 5–6 pages of theChallenging book. This ensures your child truly understands the topic and canhandle slightly tougher questions — not just the straightforward ones.

## How to Sit With Your Child

Give your child 5–10 minutes per page to attempt it on theirown while you sit beside them. Your presence alone goes a long way in keepingthem focused. When you notice their attention drifting, gently bring them back.

Keep the atmosphere light. We do not want childrenassociating studying with tension or fear of being scolded — that shapes howthey view academics for a long time. Be warm, have a bit of a laugh, and take5-minute breaks every 30 minutes.

A tip that works really well: tell your child a story in agenre they love, split into 10 parts, each ending on a cliffhanger. Reveal onepart every 30 minutes or at each break. Alternatively, a quick two-player cardgame like Uno works great — it resets their energy without taking too long.

One thing to avoid during breaks: screen time. It isgenuinely very hard for children to stop mid-video and come back, and even whenthey do, their focus is not fully there. All the momentum you built gets lost.Stick to stories or games instead.

## Going Through the Questions They Got Wrong

After the 10 minutes, go through the questions they couldnot do or got wrong. This is the most important part of the entire session —and where real learning happens.

Ask your child to read the full question out loud, thenexplain each sentence back to you in their own words. What does this sentencemean? What information is it giving us? Get them to write everything down intheir working space before attempting the solution.

To show you exactly what this looks like in practice, hereis a full walkthrough of a typical question.

## A Worked Example: Breaking Down a Question Together

The question: *"Mrs Tan had ¾ kg of sugar. She used1/5 of it to make a cake. How much sugar did she have left?"*

Ask them to read the question aloud, then go back to thevery first sentence.

### Understanding What the Question Is Saying

*"What does 'Mrs Tan had ¾ kg of sugar' mean?"*

If they are unsure, break it down piece by piece. There aretwo concepts here: ¾ and kg. Take them one at a time.

Start with kg. Keep the language simple and relatable:

*"kg stands for kilograms. It is how we measure howheavy something is. You are 45kg! Can you guess how heavy I am?"*

Have a little laugh about their answer. Then continue:

*"Fun fact — 1kg is actually 1000g. So kg and g bothmeasure weight, but we use grams for lighter things, because the number in kgwould be really tiny. An eraser, for example, is much easier to measure ingrams."*

Write it down so they can see it: 1kg = 1000g. Then testthem straight away:

*"So for lighter things, do we use kg or grams?"*

This is something we train all OPUS Tuition tutors to do —always explain simply, and always follow up with a question. It keeps the childactively thinking instead of passively listening, so information does not go inone ear and out the other.

### Explaining Fractions

Now move to ¾. Start by addressing the concept of fractionsgenerally before applying it to the question.

*"¾ is a fraction. What is a fraction?"*

If they explain it incorrectly — for example, thinking itmeans 3 minus 4, or 3 plus 4 — address that directly and clearly. Then use acompletely different number to demonstrate the concept, so they are learningthe idea rather than just copying the specific numbers in front of them.

*"Let's use 3/10 as an example. 3/10 is also a...?Yes, a fraction!"*

Use visuals as much as possible. Ask them to draw ratherthan drawing for them:

*"Can you draw a rectangle for me? Now look at thefraction — what number do you see at the bottom?" (They answer: 10.)"Good job! That means the rectangle has 10 equal pieces. Can you split itinto 10 equal pieces for me?"*

Let them draw slowly. Once they have:

*"This bottom number is called the denominator. Canyou repeat that? The denominator is the total number of parts. So in 3/10, whatis the total number of parts? Good, 10! Can you tell me what a denominator isagain?"*

Testing them again straight after is intentional — this isactive recall, and it is one of the most effective ways to make somethingstick. Keep a mental list of key concepts to revisit every couple of questionsthroughout the session: what is the denominator, what does kg stand for, howmany grams equal 1kg, and so on.

*"What about the top number? That is the numerator.The numerator tells us how many parts we actually want. For example, if I ate3/10 of my cake — can you colour in how many pieces I ate?"*

Affirm them warmly at every step. "Good job!","Brilliant!", "Fantastic!" — switch it up so it does notfeel repetitive. As a small bonus, it naturally expands their vocabulary too.When children feel good about putting in effort, they are far more motivated tokeep going.

Now bring it back to the original question. Apply the sameprocess to ¾ — ask them to draw the model and label it. Then explain thatbecause the question says ¾ kg, all four parts together equal 1kg. Ask them todraw that and label it clearly.

### Finding One Unit

*"So how many units are there in total?" (4.)"And all 4 units equal?" (1kg.) "So how do I find oneunit?"*

Here, a child whose division foundation is not yet strongmight say "minus". That is okay — acknowledge the attempt and use anew, engaging example to explain division before coming back to the question.This is important: you always want to use a different example to explain aconcept, so they understand the idea itself and can then apply it on their own.

*"Okay, good try! Let's think about this differently.Imagine I have 12 Labubus and I want to give you and your brothers equalnumbers. Can I just take 12 minus 3? Then everyone only gets one — what happensto the 9 left?"*

Let them think. They might work out something like "Ikeep giving one to each person until I run out" — that is essentiallydivision, so tell them they are on the right track. If they shrug, keepprompting with questions. At no point should you give them the answer withoutthem trying first. If they learn that you will always provide it, they stopthinking for themselves — and that habit is very hard to undo.

*"So if I give one Labubu to each person and that isone set — how many are in one set? (3.) How many sets are inside 12? I keepminusing 3 many many times. When I minus something many times, what operationdo I use — plus, minus, times, or divide?"*

Guide them to the answer through questions. Once they getthere:

*"Exactly! So divide means I minus many many times.If I take 10 divided by 2, it means: how many 2s are there in 10? What about 9divided by 3 — what does that mean?"*

Ask them to explain it back to you. They should saysomething like "how many 3s are there in 9." If they cannot, give asmany examples as needed until they can explain it in their own words. Then makeit visual:

*"Can you show me with your fingers how many timesyou can minus 3 from 9?" (Hold up 9 fingers, put down 3 at a time, counteach set together: "1... 2... 3!") "So how many 3s are there in9? Good! So if I have 12 Labubus and want to share them equally, what is thenumber sentence? Plus, minus, times, or divide? What divided by what?"*

Let them write it down and work it out, using their fingersif needed.

### Back to the Question: Finding 1 Unit

*"Okay! So now — if 4 units = 1kg, how do we find 1unit?"*

They might now ask: "But 1 cannot minus 4 at all —how?" That is actually a great observation — acknowledge it.

*"Good catch! So remember — 1kg equals to what? Yes,1000g! So since I cannot take 1kg minus 4, what do I do instead? Can you writea number sentence: 1kg equals how many grams?"*

*"So to find 1 unit, what is my number sentence?Plus, minus, times, or divide? What divided by what?"*

Always prompt for a number sentence — it is a habit worthbuilding early, as it helps children clinch method marks in exams.

Ask them to write it down and do the long division to theside. If long division is new to them, do not skip over it — use a similarexample first (e.g. 1200 ÷ 5), work through it together explaining each step,then give them one to try alone (e.g. 1500 ÷ 2). Keep giving examples at asimilar difficulty level until they can complete one independently beforereturning to the original question.

### Finding 3 Units

*"So now I know 1 unit = 250g. Can you read the firstsentence of the question again? So how many parts do I actually want? And if Iknow 1 unit = 250g, how do I find 3 units — plus, minus, times, ordivide?"*

If multiplication is unfamiliar, use the same approach:explain why it is not plus, demonstrate multiplication as adding a number manytimes using a new example, then come back to the question and ask them to writethe correct number sentence.

*"Brilliant! Can you write the number sentence down?So now — how much sugar does Mrs Tan have?"*

Once they have found 750g, ask them to draw a new model withthe total labelled as 750g. This is their working model for the second part ofthe question.

### The Second Part of the Question

*"Can you read the second sentence for me,please?"*

*"She used 1/5 of it. So how much sugar does Mrs Tanhave in total? And which model shows that? Good — the second one. What fractiondid she use? So how many parts do I split this model into? And she used howmany? Can you shade that in for me?"*

*"So if she used 1 unit — how many units does shehave left? Yes, 4! So I know 5 units = 750g. Can you write that down? And can Ifind 1 unit?"*

Notice the pattern: key information keeps getting revisited.Because new information is coming in at every step, old information can easilybe forgotten. Asking the same questions — "how much sugar does shehave?", "what is the total?" — keeps everything fresh andreinforces understanding rather than just short-term recall.

After this point, the remaining steps follow the sameprocess they have already worked through. Let them complete it on their own.That moment of independent completion — after so much guided practice — isexactly where confidence gets built.

## If Your Child Is Really Struggling

The walkthrough above assumes your child is finding thetopic quite difficult. If they just need light revision at home, the processwill be much faster — it is unlikely they cannot do any of the questions, soyou will only need to guide them through selected ones.

However, if your child really cannot complete most or all ofthe questions, skip the independent 10-minute attempt and work througheverything together from the start using the same methods above.

This is completely normal. School classrooms move at ageneral pace, and while teachers do their best, some children need moreone-on-one time to fully absorb certain concepts. That is not a reflection ofyour child's ability — it just means they need a bit more support right now,and going through things slowly and thoroughly is time very well spent.

## Quick Reference: Techniques to Keep in Mind

These can be a lot to track at first, but with time theywill become second nature. Here is a summary to refer back to:

•       Followthe school's curriculum — stay in sync with what the teacher is covering

•       Askquestions constantly — never give the answer without them trying first

•       Usevisuals — draw models, and let them draw rather than doing it for them

•       Breakconcepts down — use simple language and relatable, everyday examples

•       Alwaysuse a different example to explain a concept before coming back to the originalquestion

•       Activerecall — revisit key concepts every couple of questions throughout the session

•       Alwaysask for a number sentence — it builds method marks in exams

•       Affirmand encourage — keep the energy warm, positive, and light

•       Noscreen time during breaks — use stories or card games instead

•       5-minutebreaks every 30 minutes — consistency matters more than cramming
